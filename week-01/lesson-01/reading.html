Introduction to Computers

Before you touch a mouse, keyboard or spreadsheet, you need a clear picture of what a computer is, what it does with information, and which type of computer fits which job. This reading gives you that picture in plain language.

Reading progress

A computer is a tool for handling information. It can take in facts, work on them, show the result and keep it for later. Once you understand that simple cycle, every device — from a phone in your pocket to a server in a bank — starts to make sense.

Section 01What is a computer?

A computer is an electronic machine that accepts data, processes it according to a set of instructions called a program, produces useful information, and can store that information for later.

Key idea

A computer is defined by what it does — input, processing, output and storage — not by how it looks. A phone, a laptop, a cash machine and the control unit inside a washing machine are all computers.

People often point at a monitor and call it “the computer”. The monitor is only the screen that shows the output. The actual computer — the processor, memory and storage — may be in a separate case under the desk, folded into a laptop, held in your hand, or hidden inside another machine.

A computer is also not the same as any machine. A bicycle, a candle and a wooden ruler are useful tools, but they do not accept data, follow instructions or produce information. A computer must be able to follow a program and work with data.

Section 02The four jobs every computer does

Whatever its size or price, every computer performs the same four jobs. Together they are called the information cycle — or the input–process–output–storage cycle.

Step 1

Input

Data enters the computer through a keyboard, mouse, touchscreen, microphone, scanner or sensor.

→
Step 2

Process

The processor follows instructions: calculating, sorting, checking, formatting or searching.

→
Step 3

Output

The result is presented on a screen, printed on paper, played through speakers or sent to another device.

Storage sits alongside the cycle: the result can be saved, then brought back later as new input.

Data is raw material — numbers, words, images or sounds with no meaning yet. Information is data that has been processed into something useful. The numbers 24, 18, 30 and 30 are data. A spreadsheet that calculates an average attendance of 25.5 and labels it “weekly average” has turned that data into information.

Try it now

Think of the last message you sent on a phone. Which part was input? Which part did the phone process? What was the output? Where was it stored?

Section 03Where computers are used at work

Computers are not only for IT departments. Almost every workplace uses them to keep records accurate, communicate quickly and make better decisions. The device changes with the job; the four jobs do not.

BankAccounts, transfers, statements and fraud checksDesktop, server
Hospital or clinicPatient records, appointments, scans and test resultsDesktop, workstation, server
SchoolLesson materials, registers, results and communicationLaptop, desktop, tablet

Workplace tip

Employers rarely ask “which button do I press?”. They ask for accurate records, clear documents and messages that make sense. Knowing why a computer is used helps you choose the right tool and avoid costly mistakes.

Section 04Types of computers

Computers are grouped by size, power, mobility and purpose. These categories overlap: a high-spec laptop can be more powerful than an old desktop, and a smartphone is a computer with a phone built in. What matters is which type fits the task.

Desktop Fixed

A complete computer with a separate screen, keyboard and mouse, designed to stay on a desk.

Best for: offices, reception, school labs, shared workstations.Watch out: not portable; needs mains power.

Laptop Portable

Screen, keyboard, pointing device, battery and computer combined in one foldable case.

Best for: staff who move between desks, students and client visits.Watch out: smaller screen; harder to upgrade; easy to steal if left unattended.

Tablet Touch

A flat touchscreen computer, usually with an on-screen keyboard and long battery life.

Best for: counters, field forms, reading and simple presentations.Watch out: slower typing; fewer ports; not ideal for long reports.

Smartphone Pocket

A pocket-sized computer with mobile network, camera, GPS and thousands of possible apps.

Best for: calls, email, maps, mobile money and photos on the move.Watch out: small screen; awkward for spreadsheets and long documents.

Workstation High power

A high-performance desktop built for demanding professional work.

Best for: video editing, design, engineering and large data analysis.Watch out: expensive; needs a skilled user and good support.

Server Shared

A powerful computer that provides files, email, websites or databases to other computers on a network.

Best for: shared records, company email and websites.Watch out: not used at a desk; needs backup, security and an administrator.

Embedded system Hidden

A small computer built inside another machine to control it.

Best for: washing machines, cars, ATMs, traffic lights and medical devices.Watch out: usually cannot run other software; it has one dedicated job.

Mainframe Specialist

A very large, powerful computer that handles enormous numbers of transactions at the same time.

Best for: banks, airlines and national records.Watch out: expensive and specialised; housed in a data centre.

Power and price vary widely within each category. A new laptop can outperform an old desktop. Compare the actual specifications, not just the label.

Common mistake

“The most expensive device is always the best.” Not true. A tablet is better than a workstation at a café counter, and a desktop is often better value than a laptop for a receptionist who never moves.

Section 05Choosing the right device for the job

When an organisation buys computers, the decision should start with the work, not the brand. Ask five questions: Where will it be used? What software must it run? How many people will use it? Is it portable or shared? What is the budget for buying, maintaining and securing it?

SituationBest fitWhy
A design studio edits high-definition videoWorkstationNeeds strong processing, memory and graphics performance

A shop assistant checks stock on the shop floorTabletPortable touchscreen for simple forms and lookups

Section 06Key terms to remember

These words will come back in every lesson. Copy them into your notebook and write one example of your own for each.

Computer
An electronic machine that accepts data, processes it using instructions, produces information and can store it.
Data
Raw facts, figures, words, images or sounds before they are processed.
Information
Data that has been processed into a useful result.
Input
Data or commands entered into a computer.
Processing
The work the computer does on data by following instructions.
Output
The result a computer presents to the user.
Storage
Keeping data or files for later use, even after the power is switched off.
Hardware
The physical parts of a computer that you can touch.
Software
The programs and instructions that tell the hardware what to do.
Server
A computer that provides files or services to other computers on a network.
Embedded computer
A small computer built inside another machine to control it.
Portable device
A computer designed to be carried and used on the move, such as a laptop, tablet or smartphone.

Section 07Check your understanding

Answer each question in your notebook first, then open the answer to check yourself.

1. In your own words, what is a computer?
Example answer: A computer is an electronic machine that takes in data, works on it using instructions, produces a result and can store that result. It is defined by these jobs, not by whether it has a screen or sits on a desk.
2. Name the four jobs every computer performs and give one example of each.
Input (typing a name), processing (adding a column of numbers), output (a monitor showing the total) and storage (saving the file to a folder).
3. Is a smartphone a computer? Explain your answer.
Yes. It has a processor, memory and storage, takes input through its touchscreen and microphone, produces output on its screen and speakers, and runs programs. Making calls is only one of its jobs.
4. A clinic needs patient records shared by six staff computers. Which type of computer fits best, and why?
A server. It provides central storage and services to many computers on a network, so staff see the same records and the clinic can manage backups and security in one place.
5. Why is storage important even though a computer also has processing and output?
Processing and output are temporary: when the power goes off, unsaved work is lost. Storage keeps files and data for later, so work can be reopened, shared and backed up.

Section 08Lesson summary

  • A computer accepts data, processes it using instructions, produces information and stores it.
  • Every computer performs the information cycle: input → processing → output, with storage alongside.
  • Computers are used in offices, banks, clinics, schools, shops, government, farms and media — the device changes, the four jobs do not.
  • The main types are desktop, laptop, tablet, smartphone, workstation, server, embedded system and mainframe.
  • Choose a device by asking where it will be used, what software it must run, who will use it, whether it must be portable or shared, and what the budget allows.
