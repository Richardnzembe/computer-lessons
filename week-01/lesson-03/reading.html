Starting up, Windows 10/11 & the desktop

Lesson 2 opened the case. This lesson switches the machine on. You will learn what happens between pressing the power button and seeing the desktop, how Windows 10 and 11 differ, and how to move around the screen quickly and safely.

Reading progress

Lesson 1 explained what a computer does. Lesson 2 explained the parts and programs that do it. This lesson is about using the operating system in front of you: switching on, signing in, understanding the Windows desktop, and moving between apps and windows without losing work.

Section 01Starting up and signing in

When you press the power button, the computer does not jump straight to the desktop. It follows a start-up sequence — usually called booting — in a fixed order:

  1. Firmware check. The system firmware (UEFI, or the older BIOS) tests the main hardware — processor, memory, storage, keyboard and display. This is the POST, the power-on self-test.
  2. Boot loader. The firmware finds the operating system on the storage drive and hands control to it.
  3. Windows loads. The operating system starts its core, loads drivers for the hardware, and starts the services it needs.
  4. Sign-in screen. Windows shows the accounts on the computer and asks for the password, PIN, fingerprint or face.
  5. Desktop. After you sign in, Windows loads your personal settings, desktop, taskbar and startup apps.

Key idea

Signing in does more than unlock the screen. It tells Windows who you are, so it can load your files, your settings and your permissions. That is why shared computers need separate accounts.

Windows offers several ways to leave the computer. They are not interchangeable, and choosing the wrong one creates problems for the next person — or for your own unsaved work.

Windows sign-in and power options
OptionWhat it doesUse it when…
Sign inUnlocks your account and loads your desktop and filesYou arrive at the start of your shift
LockKeeps your session and programs open behind the sign-in screenYou step away from the desk — press Windows + L
Sign outCloses your programs and ends your session, returning to the sign-in screenAnother person will use the same computer
SleepSaves power but keeps your session in memory; wakes quicklyA short break where you want to resume instantly
RestartShuts down and starts againUpdates need to finish, or the computer is misbehaving
Shut downCloses programs and turns the computer off safelyThe end of the day, or before unplugging the machine

Never cut the power to shut down

Pressing and holding the power button, or switching off at the wall, stops the computer mid-write. Unsaved work is lost and files can be damaged. Use Start → Power → Shut down. The power button should only be held when the machine is completely frozen and you have no other option.

At a shared desk, lock before you leave. It takes one second, keeps your programs running, and stops anyone reading your email or changing your work while your back is turned.

Section 02Windows 10 and Windows 11

Both versions are Microsoft Windows. They run the same programs and store files in the same way, so the skills transfer. The differences are mostly in where things sit on the screen.

FeatureWindows 10Windows 11
Start menu positionBottom-left of the taskbarCentred by default (can be moved left)
LookSquare corners, flatter iconsRounded corners and softer colours
Quick settingsAction Centre slides out from the rightQuick Settings panel for Wi-Fi, volume and brightness
Snap layoutsSnap two windows with the mouse or keysHover the Maximise button to choose a grid of layouts
Extra toolsCortana, TimelineWidgets panel, Chat/Teams, multiple desktops made easier
SupportFree support ended in October 2025Supported and updated

Workplace note

Windows 10 reached the end of free support on 14 October 2025. It still runs, but it no longer receives routine security updates unless the organisation pays for Extended Security Updates. If your workplace still uses Windows 10, ask the IT contact about the upgrade plan — this lesson works on both versions.

Windows also comes in editions. Home covers everyday use. Pro adds business tools such as domain join, BitLocker encryption and remote desktop. Most offices use Pro; most home laptops come with Home. To check, open Settings → System → About.

Section 03The desktop and the Start menu

The desktop is the main work area you see after signing in. It is not the whole screen: it is the background layer on which windows open. Its two main parts are the wallpaper and the icons.

Start button Main entrance

Opens the Start menu: pinned and recent apps, all apps, settings, your account and the power options.

Open it: click it or press the Windows key.Windows 11: centred by default.

Search Fastest route

Type a few letters to find apps, settings, files and web results without hunting through menus.

Try: press Windows, type “calc”, press Enter.Searches: apps, settings, files.

Taskbar Control strip

The bar across the bottom holding Start, Search, pinned apps, open windows and the notification area.

Open apps: click once.Pin: right-click an app → Pin to taskbar.

Notification area Status

The right-hand zone with the clock, network, volume, battery and notification icons.

Check: Wi-Fi, sound, battery.Click: the clock to open the calendar.

Desktop icons Shortcuts

Small pictures that open a program, folder or file. This PC and the Recycle Bin are the usual ones.

Open: double-click.Arrange: right-click the desktop → Sort by / View.

Recycle Bin Safety net

Deleted files wait here until you empty it. Restore a file by right-clicking it and choosing Restore.

Empty it: right-click → Empty Recycle Bin.Careful: emptying is permanent.

The Start menu is the map of the computer. In Windows 11 it has a search box at the top, Pinned apps in a grid, Recommended recent files, and an All apps button. At the bottom sit your account name and the Power button. In Windows 10 the same parts are arranged in a tall left-hand list.

Try it now

Press the Windows key and type the first three letters of an app you use — for example “wor” for Word. Notice that Search finds it without opening any menus. Press Esc to close the search.

Right-clicking is how Windows exposes the actions for whatever you point at. Right-click the desktop for display and sorting options; right-click a file for Open, Rename, Copy and Delete; right-click a taskbar app for its jump list of recent files.

Section 04The taskbar and working with windows

Almost everything you do happens inside a window. A window has a title bar at the top with the app name and the three control buttons at the right. Learning those controls, and the shortcuts beside them, is the difference between fighting the screen and controlling it.

ControlShortcutWhat it does
Minimise (−)Windows + M (all windows)Hides the window in the taskbar; the app keeps running
Maximise (□)Double-click the title barFills the screen with the window
Restore downDouble-click the title bar againReturns the window to its previous size
Close (×)Alt + F4Closes the window and usually the program; asks you to save
SnapWindows + ← / →Places the window on the left or right half of the screen
Switch appsAlt + TabCycles through open windows; release to choose
Task ViewWindows + TabShows every open window and any extra desktops
Show desktopWindows + DHides all windows so you can see the desktop

To move a window, drag its title bar. To resize it, drag an edge or corner while the pointer becomes a double arrow. In Windows 11, hovering over the Maximise button opens Snap layouts: choose a layout and the window takes its place, then pick another window for the remaining space.

Workplace tip

Use two windows side by side for real work: a spreadsheet on the left and an invoice on the right, or a source document beside the report you are writing. Snapping them takes two seconds and removes the constant clicking between full-screen windows.

Task View also lets you create a virtual desktop — a second, separate workspace. Keep email and chat on one desktop and your focused work on another. Switch with Windows + Ctrl + ← / → and close a desktop with the × above it in Task View.

Section 05Settings, comfort and safety

The Settings app is the control centre for the computer. Open it with Windows + I or from Start. The categories you will use most in an office are:

CategoryWhat you change there
System → DisplayBrightness, scale, resolution, multiple monitors, Night light
System → SoundOutput device, volume, microphone input
PersonalisationWallpaper, colours, lock screen, taskbar and Start layout
Time & languageDate, time zone, keyboard language, region
AccountsYour sign-in options, picture, family and other users
Windows UpdateSecurity and feature updates; update history and restart options
Privacy & securityCamera, microphone and app permissions; virus protection

A comfortable setup protects your eyes, back and wrists. It also protects the organisation’s data.

  • Screen at eye level, about an arm’s length away, with the top of the screen just below eye height.
  • Reduce glare — avoid a window directly behind or in front of the screen; use Night light in the evening.
  • Follow 20-20-20 — every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds.
  • Keep wrists flat and feet supported; take a short standing break every hour.
  • Lock the screen whenever you leave the desk, even for a minute.
  • Install updates when prompted, and restart so they finish.

Common mistake

“I will just close the lid — it shuts down.” Closing the lid normally puts a laptop to sleep, not off. It still uses battery, and updates may not install. At the end of the day, use Start → Power → Shut down when the laptop will be stored or unplugged.

If a program freezes, wait a few seconds, then close it with the ×. If it will not close, press Ctrl + Shift + Esc to open Task Manager, select the program and choose End task. Restart only if the whole machine is stuck.

Section 06Key terms to remember

Copy these into your notebook. You will use them every day on a Windows computer.

Boot
The start-up sequence from pressing the power button to the desktop.
Firmware (UEFI / BIOS)
The low-level software that tests the hardware and finds the operating system.
POST
Power-on self-test: the firmware’s check of the main hardware at start-up.
Sign in
Unlocking your account so Windows loads your desktop, files and permissions.
Lock
Hiding your session behind the sign-in screen while programs keep running.
Sign out
Ending your session and closing your programs for the next user.
Sleep
A low-power state that keeps your open session in memory and wakes quickly.
Shut down
Closing programs and turning the computer off safely.
Desktop
The main work area behind the windows, holding the wallpaper and icons.
Icon
A small picture that opens a program, folder or file.
Shortcut
A link to a program or file that does not move the original.
Start menu
The main menu for apps, settings, account and power options.
Taskbar
The bar holding Start, Search, pinned apps, open windows and status icons.
Notification area
The right-hand taskbar zone with the clock, network, volume and notifications.
Window
The framed area in which an app or document runs.
Title bar
The top strip of a window with its name and minimise, maximise and close buttons.
Snap
Placing a window against a screen edge so it fills half or a quarter of the screen.
Virtual desktop
A separate workspace that keeps groups of windows apart.
Recycle Bin
Where deleted files wait until restored or permanently removed.
Settings
The Windows control centre for display, sound, accounts, updates and privacy.

Section 07Check your understanding

Answer each question in your notebook first, then open the answer to check yourself.

1. Describe what happens between pressing the power button and seeing the desktop.
The firmware runs the POST and tests the hardware; the boot loader finds Windows on the drive; Windows loads its core and drivers; the sign-in screen appears; after you sign in, Windows loads your personal settings and shows the desktop.
2. When would you lock the computer, and when would you sign out?
Lock when you are stepping away briefly and want to keep your programs open. Sign out when another person will use the same computer under their own account, because signing out ends your session and closes your programs.
3. What is the fastest way to open a program, and what else can Search find?
Press the Windows key and type the program’s name, then press Enter. Search also finds settings, files and web results, so it can replace hunting through menus.
4. How can you place two windows side by side, and how can you switch between them?
Use Windows + ← and Windows + → to snap the windows to each half, or drag a window to the left or right edge. Switch with Alt + Tab, click the taskbar buttons, or open Task View with Windows + Tab.
5. Name two display settings worth adjusting, and two habits that protect the computer and its data.
Display settings: brightness, scale, resolution, Night light, or the arrangement of a second monitor. Safety habits: lock the screen when leaving the desk, shut down properly, install updates, and keep your password private.

Section 08Lesson summary

  • Booting runs from firmware to Windows to the sign-in screen and desktop.
  • Lock keeps your work open; sign out ends your session; sleep saves power; shut down turns the computer off safely.
  • Windows 10 and 11 run the same programs; Windows 11 moves Start to the centre and adds Snap layouts, Widgets and Quick Settings. Free Windows 10 support ended in October 2025.
  • The desktop holds the wallpaper and icons; the Start menu and Search are the fastest ways to find apps and settings; the taskbar shows pinned apps, open windows and status.
  • Window controls — minimise, maximise, restore, close — and shortcuts such as Snap, Alt + Tab, Task View make work faster.
  • Settings controls display, sound, personalisation, accounts, updates and privacy; a comfortable, locked and updated computer protects both the user and the data.

Before you move on

You should be able to start up and shut down safely, find any app through Start or Search, and arrange two windows side by side. If any of those still feels uncertain, re-read Sections 1 and 4 before starting the slides.
